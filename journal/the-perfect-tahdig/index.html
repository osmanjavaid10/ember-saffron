The perfect tahdig, according to my mother

Four rules for the golden crust everyone at the table is secretly waiting for.

By Darius Mehrani

In every Persian family there is one person who is trusted with the tahdig. In ours it was my mother, and she would not let anyone else near the pot until I was twenty-six.

Tahdig means "the bottom of the pot": the golden crust of rice that forms underneath while the rest steams. Turning it out whole, a perfect gleaming disc, is the moment the whole table waits for. Getting it wrong is the moment nobody mentions again.

What actually matters

  1. Aged rice. Basmati aged for at least a year cooks drier and holds its shape.
  2. Par-boil and rinse. Six minutes in heavily salted water, then cold water to stop it.
  3. Fat, and patience. Butter and a little yoghurt in the pan, then forty minutes on the lowest heat you have. Do not lift the lid.
  4. The cloth. Wrap the lid in a tea towel to catch the steam so the crust stays crisp.

The crust forms from the bottom up. You hear it before you see it: a slow, steady crackle, like rain on a tent.

At the restaurant

We cook ours in heavy copper pans over the edge of the hearth, where the heat is gentle and even. It goes to the table whole, with quince khoresh and slow-cooked chicken, and we leave the arguments about who gets the middle piece to you.
